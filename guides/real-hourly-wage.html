How to work out your real hourly wage

The number on your contract is not what an hour of your time earns. Once you take out tax, add the hours you spend getting to work, and count what work costs you, the real figure is usually much lower.

Knowing your real hourly wage makes every price easier to judge. A ₱1,500 jacket feels different when you know it costs you 18 hours instead of 10.

Step 1: Start with your gross hourly rate

Divide your monthly pay by the hours you work in a month.

gross hourly = monthly pay ÷ (workdays per month × hours per day)

Example: ₱25,000 a month, 22 workdays, 8 hours a day. That is 176 hours, so ₱25,000 ÷ 176 = ₱142.05 an hour.

Step 2: Use what you take home

You pay for things with the money that reaches your account, not your gross pay. Multiply by your take-home percentage. If you keep 82% after tax and contributions:

₱142.05 × 0.82 = ₱116.48 an hour

Not sure of your percentage? The take-home percentage guide shows how to read it off your payslip.

Step 3: Count the hours you don't get paid for

Commuting, getting ready, and unpaid overtime are hours you give to your job. Add them to your monthly hours.

Example: 90 minutes of travel a day for 22 days is 33 extra hours. Your take-home pay of ₱20,500 is now spread over 209 hours:

₱20,500 ÷ 209 = ₱98.09 an hour

Step 4: Take out what work costs you

Fares, fuel, parking, work clothes and lunches you would not otherwise buy all come out of your pay. Subtract them first.

Example: ₱3,000 a month in work costs leaves ₱17,500.

₱17,500 ÷ 209 = ₱83.73 an hour

The same job pays ₱142 an hour on paper and ₱84 an hour in real life. That gap is why a purchase can feel cheap and still take a long time to pay off.

Which number should you use?

  • For everyday spending decisions, use your take-home rate (step 2). It is simple and honest.
  • For big choices, like a car or a job offer with a longer commute, use the full real rate (step 4).
